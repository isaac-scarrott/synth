## The first tab's fill runs under the sidebar, not up against it

In tabs mode the strip's first tab meets the sidebar's rounded right edge (`--radius-panel`, 20px).
The tab's fill stopped square at that seam, leaving a wedge of panel colour between the straight
edge and the curve. At a glance the tab read as clipped — as if the sidebar had cut a corner off it.

The first attempt rounded the strip's own top-left with `--radius-app` so the fill flowed into a
radius of its own. It fixed the hard corner and nothing else: two curves with a gap between them
still show you exactly where the fill ends. The tab still looked like it stopped short.

What it should do is not end at all. The fill continues past the seam and the sidebar's corner is
what ends it — the tab reads as a surface the sidebar sits on top of, which is what the z-order
already says. Nothing about the sidebar changes; the occlusion does the work.

The mock can't do that with the tab alone. `.content` and `.tabstrip__tabs` both clip their
overflow, so a negative margin on `.tab:first-child` gets cut at the same seam it's trying to cross.
So the run-on is a bare `.tab-bleed` block inside `.app`, 20px wide at the sidebar's trailing edge,
and `.sidebar` takes `position: relative; z-index: 1` so it stacks over it. Two mutually exclusive
states — open, and hovered-while-not-open — so the cascade resolves the bleed exactly as the tab's
own background resolves.

Native has no such clip: SwiftUI doesn't clip to bounds and `RootView` already gives the sidebar
`.zIndex(1)`, so the run-on hangs off the tab itself as a leading background offset by
`-Theme.radiusPanel`. That's the better shape, and it's why `TabChip` now routes its background
through a single `fill` property that the run-on also reads. Active, hover and the run-on are one
expression — they can't drift apart as states get added.

One knock-on, an old divergence the fix surfaced. `.tab:hover` out-specified `.tab--active` (0,2,0
against 0,1,0), so in the mock hovering the open tab washed its raised fill back down to the hover
tint; Swift, which branches on `isActive` first, never did. Settled in Swift's favour — an open tab
holding its fill under the pointer is the correct read, since the wash means "you could open this"
and there's nothing to open. Scoped to `.tabstrip__tabs > .tab:not(.tab--active):hover` so a
cluster's chips, which carry their own fill and never took the wash, stay untouched.

Verification worth recording: the native hover state could not be driven. A synthetic `mouseMoved`
posted straight to the pid — the technique that works for keys on a window parked on another Space
— doesn't reach an inactive window's `NSTrackingArea`, so `.onHover` never fires and the screenshot
comes back un-hovered. Both mock states were confirmed with a real CDP cursor; the native hover
rests on the single-`fill` structure instead of a pixel.

## Device previews are drawn from real hardware, and show the page as the phone's browser would

Both surfaces that draw a phone — the simulator session and the browser's device mode — drew a
generic black rounded rectangle: one fixed 232×476 box with a screen on it (the wrong aspect for
any shipping iPhone), and a frame with a decorative pill floating near the top. Nothing accounted
for the hardware. The simulator had no cutout at all and put the device's own name where iOS puts
the battery. Device mode had an island, but the page ran under it as if it weren't there, with no
status bar, no Safari, and no home indicator — so it answered "how wide is this page" and not the
question you open it for, which is what the thing looks like on the phone.

Both now render from one device model. Every number in it is the hardware's own, in the points the
device reports: bezel per edge, body and screen corner radii, status-bar height and inset, home
indicator. Because the frame lays out at true viewport size and only then scales to fit the pane,
those proportions survive at any pane width — the bezel-to-screen ratio stays the real one instead
of a constant that looks right at one size. The bezel is stored per edge rather than as a shorthand
because rotating has to walk each edge one place round: an SE's tall forehead ends up on the left,
not still on top, and the same rotation carries the island, the home button and every side button
with it. Side buttons are hardware, so they're on the rail, and only the Pro-era iPhones get an
action button.

The bigger change is that the screen now draws what the device draws. A page on a phone renders
inside Safari, so Safari is there: the tab bar at the bottom in portrait, folded into a single top
bar in landscape with the status bar dropped, which is what iOS actually does — the page's usable
height moves with the rotation, and you can see it move. iPad Safari keeps its bar and tab strip on
top; Android gets Chrome's single top bar and a gesture pill; the SE gets a home button and no
indicator to draw. The status bar carries the real trio (cellular, wifi, battery) and parts around
the island. Landscape insets the page clear of the island the way the safe area does.

The simulator is a booted device running an app, not a page, so it gets an app's chrome instead —
large title, tab bar, home indicator — and its device comes from the session's own name, so an
iPhone we don't carry in the fleet still boots on iPhone hardware rather than a guess.

The device screen is deliberately always light, in both themes: it's a separate machine showing a
light page, and following the app's appearance would be claiming something about the page that
isn't true. Safari's bar dropped its `backdrop-filter` — there's nothing behind it to blur in a
mock, and the filter broke the screen's corner clipping, leaving grey wedges in the two bottom
corners of every phone.

### The native port, and what it costs the page

`DeviceMode.swift` carries the same model the mock does — per-edge bezel, radii, status-bar
metrics, home indicator — and `DeviceChrome.swift` draws the hardware and the device's own browser
around the live engine view. Every metric is a device point times the stage's fit scale, so the
frame stays crisp at any fit instead of rasterising through a `scaleEffect`.

The consequence the mock could dodge and the app can't: with Safari's bars drawn, the page cannot
also have the space they occupy. So the CDP override now emulates `pageViewport` — the screen minus
the device browser's bars — and the strip's readout reports it. iPhone 16 reads 393 × 694 instead of
393 × 852, the SE 375 × 554 (a real SE gives Safari 553). That is the point of the change rather
than a side effect: at the old full-screen height a `100vh` hero passed a check no iPhone ever gives
that much room for, and device mode said it was fine. `browser.deviceMode` follows — `viewport` is
what the page gets, and a new `screen` carries the hardware size for anyone who wants both.

Two deliberate divergences from working.html. The status-bar trio is drawn from the mock's own SVG
rather than SF Symbols, so the shapes are the same glyphs and not lookalikes (the rule Phosphor.swift
already sets for the app's own icons). And landscape does *not* inset the page clear of the island
the way the mock's `.devui__page` does: a real iPhone hands the page the full width and leaves the
inset to `env(safe-area-inset-*)`, so cropping it here would hide exactly the bug the preview exists
to show. The island overlaps the page, as it does on the device.

Verified by driving the built app over the control socket: iPhone 16 portrait and landscape, SE,
Galaxy S25 Ultra, iPad mini, and the exit back to the full-pane engine, each screenshot in-process.
A `100vh` block in the test page lands exactly on the drawn page area, which is the proof that the
emulated viewport and the chrome agree.

## ⌘K search ranks flat — the best match is the top row

Typing into ⌘K used to bucket the results — Actions, Sessions, Branches, Projects — score each
bucket, then list the winning bucket *whole* before the next one's first row. The group with the
best single item led, and everything else it held came along with it. So the row you typed for
could be the best match in the palette and still sit six rows down, under five weaker siblings that
happened to share its heading. The ranking was honest per group and a lie overall, and it punished
exactly the query the palette is for: you know the name, you type it, you press ↵.

A query now drops grouping entirely. One list, every candidate scored against the same query, best
first, no headings and no dividers. Nothing about the scoring changed — an exact label still beats a
prefix, a prefix still beats a loose subsequence, and a ctx-path hit still can't outrank a label —
it just isn't partitioned before it's applied any more. Ties fall back to build order (actions,
then sessions, branches, projects), which is the old most-local-first instinct kept as a tiebreak
rather than as a wall.

Losing the headings costs nothing, because a row already says what it is: the icon carries the
type — branch glyph, session glyph, project chip — and the ctx path carries where it lives, so the
three `main` branches read as `synth`, `aviator-api`, `web-dashboard` without a "Branches" label
above them. The heading was spending a row to repeat what the row already showed.

Cold ⌘K is untouched. With no query there is no ranking to get wrong, and its groups are doing a
different job — Recent, then the context actions labelled by the scope they target — so they stay.
Grouping is now what the palette shows when it's telling you what's here, not when it's answering
a question you asked.

## A split's tabs are tabs again, marked by an 8px map of the split

A split's member tabs had left the tab language entirely. They rendered as a cluster of 22px chips
on `--raised` with a hairline ring, borrowed from the sidebar's split band — a second kind of thing
in a strip that should hold one. The cost wasn't only visual. A chip dropped the indicator slot
(`.tab-group .tab__ind { display: none }`), so a split member that needed input, errored or was
working showed nothing at all — the one signal the strip exists to carry. It dropped the active bar
and let the ring do double duty, dropped the hover wash (scoped to `.tabstrip__tabs > .tab`, which a
chip is not a child of), and pinned itself at `flex: 0 0 auto`, so a wide split shoved the + off the
end of the strip instead of ellipsizing like everything else.

Now a member IS a tab: 30px, same padding, same icon, same indicator, same ×, same active bar, same
hover wash, same shrink-to-fit. What divides two members is an inset hairline rather than a
full-height seam — they're one unit — and the seam that *ends* the group is the strip's ordinary one.

Grouping rides entirely on one added mark: an 8px map of the split drawn in the corner of each
member's icon, with that tab's own pane filled. Two tabs wearing the same map are the same split.
The map earns its pixels twice, because it says *which* pane as well as *grouped* — the shape is
computed from the real pane tree, so a three-way split gives the left member a full-height left half
and the other two the right-hand quarters. It subdivides in equal halves rather than by the real drag
fraction: at 8px a schematic reads and a true 62/38 does not. It sits on a plate of the tab's own
fill, the idiom the unread dot already uses on the opposite corner, so both marks fit on one 14px
icon without touching.

Four treatments were mocked (`split-tabs-design.html`): a rail along the group's top edge, a bracket
outline, a shared field, and this map. The map is the quietest of the four and that is the known
trade — with a lone tab full-screened and the split stashed underneath, nothing in the group is
selected and the map is all that marks it. It holds, but it asks the eye for more than a rail would.

Two things the change had to repair beyond the members themselves. The hover rule moves from
`.tabstrip__tabs > .tab:hover` to `.tab:hover`, since a member is no longer a direct child of the
strip. And the `.tab-bleed` rules — the first tab's fill running on under the sidebar's rounded
corner — keyed off `.tabstrip__tabs > .tab:first-child`, which stopped matching the moment a group
led the strip; they now match the group's first tab too.

## Native port: a split's tabs are tabs again, marked by the pane map

The entry above, in the app. `TabCluster`/`ClusterChip` are gone — the 22pt chip pair they drew was
the same second visual language the mock carried, with the same cost: `ClusterChip` had no
indicator slot at all, so a split member that needed input or errored showed nothing.

A member is now a `TabChip` like any other, given two extra facts. `groupPosition` decides its
seams — members hand their full-height seam to the last of the group and are divided instead by a
1pt hairline inset 8pt top and bottom — and `paneMap` gives it the mark. `AppStore.paneRects(for:)`
walks the same tree `echoMemberIDs` flattens, in the same a-before-b order, handing each session its
pane as a unit-square rect; `PaneMap` draws that at 8pt in the icon's bottom-right corner, on a plate
of the tab's own fill, opposite the unread dot.

Two things the port had to carry over beyond the members. A member's content spacing goes to 11pt
because the map overhangs the icon and the name has to clear it. And `bleedsUnderSidebar` — the
first tab's fill running on under the sidebar's rounded corner — used to be handed only to a lone
leading tab, on the reasoning that a leading cluster inset its chips and never met the seam. A
member is full-bleed now, so `TabGroup` passes the bleed down to its first member.

En route: `automation.screenshot` grew a `"window":"main"` selector. It prefers a visible NSPanel
(so a ⌘K capture contains the palette), but a tooltip is an NSPanel too — one resting over the
window silently hijacked every capture, returning a 508×38 image of the tooltip instead of the app.
The evidence verb was blind to the app in exactly the case you most want evidence.

Verified against the built app over the control socket, tabs mode on, five terminal sessions on one
branch: `automation.split` × 2 builds `(row A (col B C))`, and the strip draws A with a full-height
left half, B the top-right quarter, C the bottom-right quarter — the real tree, not a guess.
Jumping to a lone tab stashes the split (`panes: 1`) and the group holds with no member raised and
the maps still the only mark. ⌘1 makes the group's first member active and its fill runs on under
the sidebar's curve, proving the bleed survives a group leading the strip.
## Antigravity CLI (`agy`) is Synth's third hosted agent — ADR-0012's promise, cashed

**What it is.** A session can now run **Antigravity** alongside Claude Code and OpenCode: its own
create row on every surface, its own flags block in Settings, its own mark, auto-naming, liveness /
needs-input / error indicators, browser ownership and click-to-comment. Persisted `AgentID` is
`antigravity`, the binary is `agy`. ADR-0012 said a third agent would be one `AgentDescriptor` plus
one `AgentSupervisor` and nothing else; this is the entry that tests the claim, and it holds.

**Which `agy`.** Google split the product at I/O in May 2026: the terminal agent is now a Go TUI
called `agy` (v1.1.x, Claude Code's shape — `-p`, `--continue`, `--mode`, hooks), replacing Gemini
CLI, and it is *not* the Antigravity IDE from November 2025. The IDE ships a binary with the same
name — a shell launcher at `~/.antigravity/antigravity/bin/agy` pointing into
`/Applications/Antigravity.app`, whose `chat` subcommand drives the GUI and never speaks to a
terminal. On this machine that launcher sits **ahead of Homebrew on the login PATH**, so naive
which-resolution finds the wrong one and Synth would host a window-opener. Detection therefore
resolves the symlink and **rejects any candidate inside an `.app` bundle**. Path-order alone can't
tell the two apart; what they resolve to can.

**Transport: hooks, like Claude Code — but nothing of the user's is written.** Antigravity publishes
no event bus, so subscription (OpenCode's shape) isn't available; it does load
`<workspaceDir>/.agents/hooks.json`. That file is near Claude's settings but not it, and the
difference is the kind that costs an afternoon: each top-level key is a *named* hook (names merge
across configs, so Synth's never displaces the user's), tool events group their handlers under a
`matcher` while lifecycle events list them flat, and a handler must print **nothing** — `decision`
is required on a `PreToolUse` verdict, so emitting even `{}` hard-denies every tool the agent tries.
The other thing worth recording is what was verified empirically rather than read: **hooks load for
every workspace dir, including dirs appended with `--add-dir`**. So the shim's launch role creates a Synth-owned per-session
directory, writes `hooks.json` into it wiring `PreInvocation` / `PreToolUse` / `PostToolUse` / `Stop`
to `synth-hook event`, and appends `--add-dir <that dir>` to the real argv. The repo's own
`.agents/`, and the global `~/.gemini/antigravity-cli/settings.json`, are never touched — the
instrumentation lives and dies with the session. `PreInvocation` and `PostToolUse` mean working,
`Stop` means idle plus mark-unread.

Readiness is the one place Claude's rule does *not* carry over, and it took three tries to get
honest. Claude's first hook is a session start, so attaching is being ready; agy's first hook is a
*turn* start, which may be minutes away — and readiness is what lets a browser comment be pasted in,
so a row that waits for it is a row nothing can be handed to. But the obvious replacement, the log's
`Launching CLI mode`, is a lie in the other direction: agy logs it within milliseconds while the
screen is still a *sign-in spinner*, and the harness caught exactly that — the row went live at
+1.6s, the comment was pasted into the spinner, and the turn never happened. Nor is the model
resolving (~1.5s later) enough: agy spends a further ~3s re-resolving models, quota and slash
commands, redrawing as it goes, and a paste in that window is lost too.

So readiness is the model resolving **and** the log going quiet for 2s — the honest signal, because
agy publishes no "input box is live" event of any kind — capped at 20s so a log that never settles
cannot strand the row, plus the original 10s fallback narrowed to the case it was written for: a
log that never said anything at all, because the user supplied their own `--log-file`.

**The workspace trust prompt is a first-class blocked state.** On a path agy has not seen before it
opens on a modal "do you trust the contents of this project?" — which every fresh Synth worktree
hits, so this is the *first* thing most Antigravity rows do. There is no hook and no log line for
it. Left alone the row reads live-and-idle while the agent is stopped dead, and a comment delivered
then is doubly lost: the text is swallowed by the modal and the Enter that should submit it answers
the prompt instead. The supervisor therefore learns the workspace from the log line agy already
prints (`Initializing CLI store manager for workspace <path>`) and checks it against agy's own
`trustedWorkspaces`; while it is absent the row is `needsInput` and never live. Synth only ever
*reads* that record — granting filesystem trust on the user's behalf is not Synth's call to make.
`Store` had to give a little for this: its "a needs-input is only legitimate mid-turn" guard, which
exists for Claude's end-of-turn race, is now scoped to rows whose agent is already reachable — an
agent that has not yet reported ready and says it is blocked is blocked on the way *in*.

**needs-input is tailed, because no hook fires for it.** There is no permission-prompt event, but the
CLI's own log announces one ("Surfacing tool confirmation"). The shim appends `--log-file` at a
Synth-owned path (and stands down if the user supplied their own), and the supervisor tails it; the
next `PreToolUse` / `PostToolUse` / `Stop` clears the row back to working or idle. A log tail is a
weaker contract than a hook and is scoped to exactly that one signal for that reason.

**Resume, delivery, MCP.** Every hook payload carries `conversationId` (and the hook process env
carries `ANTIGRAVITY_CONVERSATION_ID`), so capture rides the same `.agentSessionCaptured` path
Claude's `session_id` does, and resume is `agy --conversation <id>` — a true conversation resume, not
a fresh turn with context. Text is delivered as a **TUI paste**, like Claude Code: there is no prompt
API to post to. Browser MCP registers per worktree at `.agents/mcp_config.json` (a `mcpServers`
object), never the global `~/.gemini/config/mcp_config.json`. The row's name comes from the same
payloads' `transcriptPath`: agy summarises its conversations into a protobuf blob inside a SQLite
file, which is no use to a hook, so the transcript's opening `<USER_REQUEST>` is what the row is
called — the first one, not the latest, so the name survives a resume instead of drifting.

**Two environment decisions.** `AGY_CLI_DISABLE_AUTO_UPDATE=1` is set, because an embedded agent
swapping its own binary mid-session breaks the session Synth is supervising. And
`ANTIGRAVITY_CONVERSATION_ID` / `ANTIGRAVITY_PROJECT_ID` are **scrubbed** on spawn: `agy` sets them for
*its* children, so without scrubbing an agent launched from inside a Synth session inherits them and
gets mistaken for the row's own agent. Un-instrumented pass-through covers the subcommands (`agents`,
`models`, `plugins`, `update`, `install`, `changelog`, `help`), `--version`, and print mode
(`-p` / `--print` / `--prompt`) — one-shot, exits, nothing to supervise, the same treatment `claude
-p` gets. Note for whoever edits the shim next: bare `agy version` is *not* a subcommand and grabs
the TTY; only `--version` is safe.

**Auth.** Google sign-in, and the free tier is enough to host a real session — quota is shared across
the Antigravity surfaces, so a heavy IDE day is felt in the terminal. Live harness gates skip
themselves when the machine isn't signed in rather than failing, since sign-in is interactive and
browser-bound; on a signed-in machine they run for real.

## Ctrl-C interrupts an opencode row, it doesn't quit it

A session vanished mid-turn. It was the holibob master row, an opencode conversation 69 messages
deep ("Review removed Stripe Elements A/B test"), and it went twice in one afternoon: at 16:54 and
again at 17:20, thirty seconds after being resumed, both times while the model was streaming.

opencode's log says what happened without ambiguity — `disposing all instances`, then the in-flight
message failing with `error=Aborted`. The shutdown came first; the abort was its consequence. That
is opencode's clean quit path, and its default keybinds are why: `app_exit` is bound to
`ctrl+c,ctrl+d,<leader>q`, while `session_interrupt` is `escape` alone. Ctrl-C mid-turn — the one
gesture every agent user has in their fingers for *stop* — quits the application. Measured under a
pty: exit code **0**.

Zero is what makes it fatal here. Synth ends a row on a clean exit (features 2026-07-06) — right for
`exit` in a shell, right for quitting an agent deliberately — so the row was closed, torn down, and
removed from the tree, taking with it the captured `ses_…` id that was the only handle back to the
conversation. And because the row was the *foreground* one, `routeTransition` raised no toast at
all: the done/closing card is only for sessions you aren't looking at. One keystroke, no confirm,
nothing on screen, no way back. The user's account of it — "it seems to have just disappeared, I'm
not too sure why" — is precisely accurate.

Claude Code interrupts on Ctrl-C. Two agents hosted in one app must not disagree about the stop
gesture, so opencode is rebound inside Synth: `session_interrupt` gains `ctrl+c`, `app_exit` keeps
`ctrl+d` and `<leader>q`. Quitting stays available, but it stops being one thumb away from an
interrupt.

The seam is `OPENCODE_TUI_CONFIG`, an env var naming an *extra* TUI config file. It merges after the
user's own `~/.config/opencode/tui.json` and before any project one, which is exactly the priority
this deserves: Synth changes a binding without writing to a file it doesn't own, and anyone who
wants the stock behaviour back can say so in a project `.opencode/tui.json`. The overlay is written
once to `<Application Support>/opencode-tui.json` and named from `decorate`, alongside the
autoupdate and terminal-title vars already set there. Keybinds are a TUI concern, not a server one —
`OPENCODE_CONFIG_CONTENT` carries no `keybinds` key and cannot do this job.

What remains true, and is deliberately left standing for now: a clean agent exit still destroys a
resumable conversation silently. Rebinding removes the trigger, not the loaded gun.

## An agent that quits parks its conversation on a Reopen card

Rebinding Ctrl-C removed the trigger, not the loaded gun. `ctrl+d` still quits opencode, `/exit`
still quits it, a crash that happens to exit 0 looks identical from outside — and every one of
those routes ended at the same place: `closeSession`, which takes the row out of the tree and the
captured `agentSessionID` with it. Synth was destroying a conversation it knew how to restore, and
it is the *only* thing that knew: opencode still holds `ses_…` in its own store, but nothing in
the app can point at it once the row is gone.

So a clean exit no longer closes an agent row that has a conversation. It parks it. The row leaves
the tree exactly as before — no dead rows accumulating in the sidebar — but it lands on an undo
card in the deck: the agent's mark, "OpenCode quit", the conversation's name underneath, and
**Reopen**. Reopen slots the row back where it stood and the pane rebuilds its terminal, which
launches with `--resume` — the same reconstruction a row gets after a restart, reached by a
different door.

**The card never drains.** Every other undo is a fuse: eight seconds, then the removal stands.
That is right when you are undoing your own gesture, because you were there for it. This card is
about something you didn't do, and it can land while you are away from the keyboard — which is
exactly the case where a countdown spends the conversation on your behalf. So it is sticky and
band 1 (standing) rather than band 0 (fused): `band` now asks whether an undo actually drains
instead of assuming every undo does. × still means "let it stand", the same thing the bar meant.

Two things it deliberately does not do. It does not fire for a row with nothing to resume — a
plain shell's `exit`, or an agent quit before its first prompt (opencode creates the conversation
lazily, so there is genuinely nothing behind it) — because the rule is "never destroy a
conversation Synth can restore", not "never close a row". And it does not restate itself: the
evidence line carries the conversation's name, dropped when the row still wears its stock one,
since "OpenCode quit / OpenCode" is the verb line twice.

The other half of the fix is that it is raised **at all**. `routeTransition` only ever escalated a
closing row you were *not* looking at, so the foreground case — the one that actually happened,
watching a turn stream and having the pane empty itself — got silence. `softDelete` raises
regardless of what is on screen, so routing the close through it fixes that as a side effect
rather than as a special case.

`t17_agent_quit` is the gate: a real opencode row, a real conversation, SIGTERM standing in for the
keystroke, then the card's shape, the round trip through Reopen, `--session <id>` on the relaunched
PTY, and the negative case that still closes outright.
