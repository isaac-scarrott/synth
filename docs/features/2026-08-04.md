# 2026-08-04


## The window is translucent, terminal included

Synth's window now sits *on* the desktop rather than in front of it: the window server blurs the
wallpaper behind it, and every surface above that is a plain alpha fill over the one blurred sample.
The reference point was a Ghostty setup at `background-opacity = 0.75` / `background-blur = 20`,
deliberately dialled back — the desktop is a wash under the app, never a subject.

**The terminal participates.** The two cheaper scopes — chrome only, or everything-but-terminal —
were rejected, and it is worth writing down why, because the terminal is the risky part and the cheap
versions are much less work. A translucent shell wrapped around an opaque black rectangle does not
read as a translucent app; it reads as a bug, because the terminal is the largest single surface in a
working pane and the eye goes straight to the one thing that did not move. Half the effect costs
nearly all of the risk to legibility and buys none of the impression.

### The blur is not an NSVisualEffectView, and that decided everything else

This started with `.underWindowBackground` — the material literally named for this position in the
stack — and it was wrong. Every AppKit material **tints as well as blurs**, and that tint multiplies
with the surface alpha above it exactly the way a second coat would. Behind the alphas the design file
had been tuned to, it left roughly 4% of the wallpaper showing and dragged light mode's near-white
down to a flat grey. Translucent on paper, invisible in the room.

So Synth asks the window server directly: `CGSSetWindowBackgroundBlurRadius`, radius 60, which adds no
tint at all. That is what CSS `backdrop-filter: blur()` does — which is why `working.html` could be
trusted to tune against in the first place — and what Ghostty does, which is where the look came from.

It is **private API**, and it is treated as a progressive enhancement rather than a dependency: the
symbols are resolved once through `dlsym`, and if they are missing `WindowBlur.isAvailable` is false,
the window stays opaque and `Theme.windowCoat` resolves to a solid colour. A macOS that drops the call
makes Synth look the way it did before any of this existed. The failure mode to avoid is not "no blur"
— it is a translucent shell over a *sharp* desktop, which is unreadable, and that is the one outcome
the fallback rules out.

### One coat, and tints on it

The blur only samples what is behind the *window*, so there is exactly one of it, at the root. That
forces the rule the rest follows: there is also exactly one **translucent coat**
(`Theme.windowCoat`), and anything that needs to differ from it is a tint *on* it, never a second
coat.

Two coats compound. A sidebar at 0.84 over a pane coat at 0.86 leaves 2% of the desktop where they
overlap and 14% where they don't, so the wallpaper is visibly more present in the pane than in the
sidebar. Worse, the sidebar's 20pt rounded corners are cut *out* of its fill, so the notch behind them
showed a hole straight through to the wallpaper with no surface underneath.

So the sidebar became `Theme.sidebarStep` — `mono(0.04, 0.025)`, a tint of the ink rather than a
colour. A fixed colour breathes with the wallpaper: the step between sidebar and pane opens and closes
as the coat rides up and down under it. A multiplicative tint holds its ratio, measuring ~9.5 levels
across the whole photo, sky to slate. `Theme.sidebar` keeps its opaque hex; all that is left for it is
the rails in ⌘? and the changelog, which sit on a dialog rather than on the coat.

### Dark carries 0.11 more opacity than light, and that gap is the finding

Not a rounding choice. The same amount of wallpaper light lands completely differently depending on
what it lands on: against light's 250 it is a ~3% change and nearly invisible; against dark's 25 it is
a ~28% *lift* and the surface goes milky. A near-equal pair read as too transparent in dark and too
flat in light **at the same time**, which is what made the asymmetry obvious. 0.86 light, 0.97 dark.

Radius and coat are separate dials and it is worth knowing which to reach for: radius decides how
*recognisable* the desktop is, and costs nothing; the coat decides how much light gets through at all,
and costs legibility. Reach for radius first. Radius 20 — Ghostty's own setting — was far too sharp
for a whole shell, and a recognisable photo reads as "too transparent" at the same alpha that a soft
wash reads as right.

### It cost one grey, and there was no alternative

`--ink-meta` had to darken 16 levels in light, #6B6E76 → #5B5E66. The tier cleared 4.5:1 with no
margin at all against the opaque sidebar (4.52:1), so *any* coat put it under — 3.56:1 over the
wallpaper's dark end. The arithmetic says raising the coat cannot fix it: the sidebar only clears 4.5
again at a fully opaque 1.00, so there is no light coat value that keeps a visible effect and the old
ramp. Moving the ink was the only move that kept both, and it lifts the tier to 6.26:1 on the opaque
dialogs and rails that also use it. Dark needed nothing — it holds 5.04:1 at the 0.97 coat.

`--text-muted` and `--text-faint` were deliberately below 4.5 already, as secondary tiers, and
degrade proportionally rather than crossing a line they were holding.

### The terminal, and the seam that gave it away

Ghostty paints the whole card now — cells *and* the `window-padding` inset that frames them — at
`background-opacity` 0.55 light / 0.58 dark. Low only because the card nests inside the coat and the
two multiply: at the chrome's own alpha the terminal would show a fraction of a percent and be opaque
in all but name. As it is, about 6% of the desktop reaches the cells in light and barely 1% in dark,
which is what keeps the photo's texture out of the text.

`TermSurface` used to paint `Theme.tuiBg` behind the ghostty view as well, and that was a genuine bug
translucency exposed: the padding band got one coat and the cells got two, so the terminal read as
*more solid than its own border*. Ghostty's inset absorbed the SwiftUI padding (`window-padding` 23/19)
and `Theme.tuiBg` is deleted — there is one painter and one alpha, and no pair of numbers to keep in
step by hand. That config is global, so the scratch terminal dropped its own 14/11 inset to match;
1–2pt off its old geometry, which is not worth a second per-surface config to reclaim.

### Two more things that had quietly depended on an opaque surface

A pattern worth naming, because all three bugs this change surfaced were the same shape: something
relying on an opaque fill to occlude what was behind it.

**The terminal card's drop shadow.** SwiftUI's `.shadow` blurs the view's *alpha*, so the moment the
card's fill stopped being opaque, its own shadow showed through it — a uniform grey wash across the
cells, 7 levels deep, measured. CSS never had the problem: an outer `box-shadow` is clipped to outside
the border-box. The halo is now cast from an opaque stand-in with the card's footprint subtracted by an
even-odd path (`OutsideRoundedRect`). A `.destinationOut` blend was tried first and punched through the
window's coat as well, dropping the card onto the bare desktop — a path subtraction composites nothing
and guesses nothing. Worth keeping rather than dropping the shadow, because on light the translucent
card composites to within a shade of the pane, so the halo and the hairline are the only things left
saying "card".

**The tabs-mode first-tab bleed.** It was a plain 20pt rectangle slid left under the sidebar, correct
only because an opaque sidebar hid everything but the corner wedge. Against a 4% tint the whole
rectangle showed through as a bright block with a hard square edge. It is now the wedge itself
(`SidebarCornerWedge`), so the fill follows the sidebar's curve and stops where the curve does — height
equal to the radius, because below the arc the sidebar's edge runs straight and there is no gap to fill.

### Prototyping it

`working.html` grew a fixed backdrop layer and a dev-only switcher — `photo` / `night` / `gradient` —
because a browser cannot sample the desktop and translucency judged against nothing is not judged at
all. The photo is the actual wallpaper this was tuned against, vendored beside the fonts for the same
reason they are (a design file that silently falls back is one you can review and be wrong about):
mid-tone sky over high-frequency slate, which stresses light and dark chrome at once. `night` is that
same image graded down rather than a second binary. None of it ports to the app.

The prototype earned its keep and also showed its limit. It caught the compounding rule, the notch and
the sidebar step; it could not predict the material's tint, and its alphas did not survive contact with
a real desktop at native resolution — dark moved 0.88 → 0.97. A CSS blur over a downscaled JPEG is a
good model of an untinted blur and a bad model of how much of it you can afford.

*Rejected:* chrome-only and everything-but-terminal scopes (above); per-pane `backdrop-filter` in the
design file, which browsers compound and no AppKit route can reproduce, so the prototype would have
promised a look the port could not deliver; `state = .followsWindowActiveState` on the material, back
when there was a material — AppKit's default drops the effect whenever the window loses focus, and on a
shell that is translucent by design that reads as the window breaking.
## Tabs-mode branches carry their session facts without growing a third tree level

Tabs mode removes session rows from the sidebar because those sessions already live in the tab
strip. That made the branch the deepest visible row, but the old compact branch shell still spent
space on a disclosure chevron and hid the session count in a tooltip. The branch now uses that space
as a 46px two-line summary: its name first, then `N sessions · activity`.

The right edge is a fixed pair of 16px slots: pull-request state, then the branch's highest-priority
session state. Empty slots remain reserved, so names and status never jump between branches. Live,
error, needs-input, unread, pending, and PR states keep their established glyphs and colours; idle
activity moves into the facts line. Hover actions cover the whole rail rather than leaving a status
glyph behind.

This is tabs-mode presentation only. Classic sidebar mode keeps its 30px disclosure row, middle
truncation, inline PR identity, and activity rail unchanged. Rename preserves the two-line tabs-mode
geometry, and the status-only rail has explicit VoiceOver labels because it is the sole sidebar
signal once session rows are absent.

The HTML source and native SwiftUI app ship the same layout. Gate: `swift build`, an independent
code-to-spec audit, tabs-on and tabs-off screenshots from isolated running app instances, and the
`working.html` / `big-picture-design.html` subset diff.

## The pre-notarization Gatekeeper check asserts *why* it was rejected

`release.sh` assesses the freshly signed app with `spctl` before sending it to Apple. That stage's
only passing answer is `rejected` / `source=Unnotarized Developer ID` — Gatekeeper is right, the
ticket does not exist yet. The check was there to catch the *other* rejections, the ones that mean
the build is broken: unsigned nested code, a missing hardened runtime. But those are also a
`rejected`, and the line ended in `|| true`, so every outcome printed the same word and continued.
A guard that cannot fail is decoration, and the one reader it had was left to either panic at
`rejected` or learn to skim past it — which is how the real failure would have gone by.

It now captures the verdict, prints it, and requires the reason to be `source=Unnotarized Developer
ID`, dying on any other rejection. The stage also labels itself in the log, so the expected
`rejected` no longer needs a source comment to interpret it. The post-stapling assess is unchanged:
that one must be `accepted`, and `pipefail` already made it fatal.

Recorded alongside it in the release skill: `release.sh` must not be edited while it runs. Bash
reads a script incrementally, so an in-place rewrite resumes execution at a byte offset that now
falls mid-line. A release is ~20 minutes mostly spent waiting on Apple, which is precisely when
editing it feels free.
## Claude Code answers for its own colours in light mode

Light-mode Claude Code was unreadable, and the first thing worth recording is that it was unreadable
for a reason nobody would guess from looking at the theme: `TerminalTheme`'s light palette was
correct, `AgentTheme` was already syncing Claude Code's `theme` key, and the light theme it selects
is mostly sound. The failure was in *when* the key is read.

**`theme` in `~/.claude.json` is read once, at startup.** Measured directly: a session was started on
`dark`, the key rewritten to `light` underneath it, and the frame forced to redraw — it stayed dark,
four seconds later too. So the old sync only ever reached *new* sessions. Flip Synth to light with
work in progress and every open session keeps painting the dark theme onto a near-white surface,
where Claude Code's body text (`#ffffff`) measures **1.06:1** and **57 of its 72 colour tokens** fall
under 4.5:1. That is the whole of "I can't read the output", and no amount of correcting the light
theme would have touched it, because the sessions were never on the light theme.

**`theme: "auto"` is worse, and is the trap.** "Auto (match terminal)" does not match the terminal.
Claude Code enables DEC mode 2031 (`\e[?2031h`, theme-change notifications) and then ignores what
arrives: fed `CSI ? 997 ; 2 n` (light), then dark, then light again, with a full repaint after each,
it stayed on the dark theme throughout — and starts dark with no answer at all. The old `AgentTheme`
guarded on `current == "dark" || current == "light"`, so an `auto` user was deliberately *skipped* and
left in exactly the unreadable state. `auto` is now adopted, on the grounds that following the
appearance is what the setting asks for and Synth actually does it.

### The lever is the custom-theme directory, because it is the only live one

Claude Code watches `~/.claude/themes` and reloads on any change. So Synth ships a theme of its own,
`custom:synth`, and re-themes by rewriting that one file — verified to re-theme a **running** session
within a second, in both directions. Three things follow from the single `base` field, which is why
it is one file and not a light/dark pair: the 72 colour tokens, the diff renderer, and the syntax
highlighter, which Claude Code picks from the *resolved base* (GitHub for light, Monokai Extended for
dark) and not from the theme's name — checked by pointing a `base: dark` custom theme at it and
reading the picker back.

Writes are conditional on content changing. `applyTheme` runs on every window move between displays,
and each needless write wakes every running session's watcher.

### What the 20 overrides are, and what was deliberately left alone

The token names and their light values were read out of Claude Code's own theme editor, one row at a
time — they are not in the binary as text, the values live in bun bytecode. Each override keeps its
token's hue and saturation and lowers only lightness: the least change that clears the floor, so
Claude Code still looks like Claude Code. Text is held to 4.5:1 and four non-text tokens to 3:1.

The worst offender was **`subtle` at 2.06:1** — the dim grey every hint, timestamp and token count is
written in, which is what "grey text on a white background" turns out to be. Then `chromeYellow`
(1.61), `promptBorder` (2.68), `professionalBlue` (2.75), and Claude's own `#d77757` (2.96).

Three left alone, each for a reason that is the finding:
- **`diffAddedWord` / `diffRemovedWord` read like foregrounds and are not.** They are the fill behind
  a changed *word* inside a diff line, and Claude Code paints black on them. Deepening them to clear
  4.5:1 as ink drove the ink *on* them from ~17:1 down to 4.28:1. A fill is corrected by what it has
  to carry, not by its own ratio against a surface it never touches. Measured, then reverted.
- **`inverseText` is white by design** and only ever painted on a fill, so the fill has to earn the
  ratio — which is why `professionalBlue`, its usual backing, is corrected instead.
- **`clawd_background` is the mascot's silhouette.** Brand artwork, not a contrast decision.

### Two things are recorded rather than fixed, because Synth holds no lever

- **The syntax highlighter is a separate palette**, selected wholesale from the base rather than per
  token. On the light surface it leaves `#0086b3` at 3.90:1 and a faint `#333333` at 4.15:1. The
  asserted passes run with `CLAUDE_CODE_SYNTAX_HIGHLIGHT=0` so the theme's own colours are measured
  in isolation rather than blamed for it.
- **One colour belongs to no theme at all.** Forcing *every* token to magenta and re-rendering left
  `#5769f7` (4.14:1, the file path in a `Bash(...)` header) untouched. That is the proof it is
  hard-coded, and the reason the gate exempts it instead of failing on it.

Dark is ridden untouched, the same asymmetry `TerminalTheme` makes: dark is what all of these colours
were chosen against. It ships two of its own failures — the `❯` prompt marker at 1.48:1 on the
composer fill, and white at 3.09:1 on a diff word highlight — so dark is *pinned* just under what it
renders today rather than gated: not a claim, a tripwire.

### `t24_agentcontrast` measures this from the bytes, not the tokens

`t13_termcontrast` gates the sixteen palette slots a terminal owns, and Claude Code uses none of
them. The new suite replays a real session through a small terminal emulator (`ttygrid`) and reads
out every run of ink with the background it sits on, because only the settled grid is what a reader
sees — a TUI sets a foreground once and then moves the cursor around for a dozen lines, and redraws a
row three times before the frame lands. `ccontrast` resolves each cell the way ghostty would,
including faint, which is a blend rather than a colour. The override table is parsed out of
`AgentTheme.swift` rather than restated, so the gate cannot pass a theme the app does not ship.

The fixture is a transcript of our own, replayed with `--resume` — no sign-in, no token spent, and
the same diff, code fence, todo list, collapsed result and failed command on every machine. Three
bugs in the harness itself were caught by one assertion, that a line only the fixture can produce is
actually on screen: without it the suite measured a **trust prompt**, whose four colours pass
everything. The causes were all path-shaped — no `chdir`, then a `/var` vs `/private/var` mismatch,
then Claude Code's project-directory encoding, which flattens *every* non-alphanumeric to `-` and not
just the separators.

Two emulator bugs came from feeding it incrementally, and both would have been invisible in a
one-shot capture: a pty read can end mid-escape-sequence (`\e[38;2;51;51;51m` split after `\e[3`
renders as `8;2;51;51;51m` inside a word) and mid-character (box drawing arriving as replacement
characters, so it no longer classified as chrome).

`AgentTheme.sync` now takes its home directory as a parameter, and it has to: `NSHomeDirectory()`
reads the password database rather than `$HOME` for a process that is not sandboxed, so a test that
exports `HOME` and calls it will rewrite the developer's own `~/.claude.json` while believing it is
working in a temp directory. It did exactly that once.


## The archive becomes a place you can stand, and the design files catch up to `ArchiveSweeper`

`ArchiveSweeper` and its two settings rows have shipped in the native app for a while; the design
files had neither. The archive existed only as a verb — a row leaving the sidebar, a folder
reclaimed "later" — with no surface listing what is still on disk and no statement of the rule that
decides when it stops being.

**Synth tab ▸ Archived worktrees.** `SettingsPane.swift`'s switch and grace picker
(Never / 7 / 14 / 30 days, default 7), plus two new rows that have no counterpart in the app:
`Most worktrees archived` (25) and `Most disk archived` (50 GB). The app bounds how long the grace
lasts; these bound what it costs.

Only the switch carries a description, and only the half of the app's line you can't read off the
controls — that the sweep touches nothing unrecoverable and never deletes the branch. The pickers
say their own rule; a sentence under "Never · 7 · 14 · 30 days" would only say it again, slower.
Turning the switch off folds the other three rows away.

**The constraint on the budgets, and the thing to check any future condition against: a budget
brings an unblocked folder's turn forward; it never lets one through a gate.** ArchiveSweeper's
invariant is that every byte it reclaims is reconstructible from a remote, so a disk cap that
evicted the oldest folder regardless would be a hole straight through it. An over-budget archive
full of unpushed work stays put and says so.

**Project tab ▸ Archived.** The per-project list: name, age, size, and the sweeper's verdict —
`commits not pushed`, `PR still open`, `6 days left`, `checking…`, with the accent reserved for the
one row about to go. Those are `ArchiveSweeper.Block`'s own strings minus its `— kept` suffix,
which a chip sitting on a row that is plainly still there does not need; ⌘K puts it back, where the
reason is flat text with no row under it to carry that.

The section states no policy in prose. It doesn't need to: the verdicts are the policy, computed
from the same three knobs the Synth tab sets, so the list cannot describe a rule the app isn't
running. Sweep off and the verdicts simply aren't there. Under the list, two ratios and no
sentence — `8 / 25 worktrees · 7.8 / 50 GB` — because a cap you can't see coming is
indistinguishable from folders vanishing at random, and which folders it called early is already on
their own rows. With no cap set, or the sweep off, the line disappears.

Four rows, scrolling, fifth visibly cut: a project with thirty archived branches must not push the
rest of Settings out of reach, and the cut is the only honest scroll cue on a list whose scrollbar
hides until touched.

**Restore and delete.** Restore puts the branch row back — archived this session it keeps its
detached node and slots back where it stood, archived earlier its row is rebuilt. Permanent delete
confirms, and confirms in the ⌘K frame: the app has one confirm surface, so Settings' trash opens
`deleteWorktreeFrame` rather than growing a dialog of its own. "Confirms from every surface" means
every surface routes there, not that each grows its own wording to keep in step — the live-branch
`Delete worktree now` and the archived entry are now literally one frame with two callers, Cancel
preselected in both. Past the confirm it parks on the same destructive undo card as every other
delete.

Two divergences fixed while here. ⌘K's archived list was reading the global array from inside a
project frame — now project-scoped like the frame containing it. And its rows restored on ↵, making
"or delete one now" in its own placeholder unreachable; they now drill into the Restore / Delete
worktree now frame `Palette.swift` has had all along.

### The native app

Ported the same day. `FolderSize` is new — nothing in the app measured a folder before, and the
size cap needs it; it walks off the main actor into an observable cache, `node_modules` included,
because a disk-usage number that skips the biggest directory is not one. `archiveMaxCount` /
`archiveMaxGB` follow the existing pref shape. `Block.chip` derives from `Block.line` by dropping
the suffix, so the 23 strings stay written once. `Verdict` gained the display strings; the verdict
map lost its `@ObservationIgnored`, without which a pane would never redraw when a tick filled it.

The budget promotes by re-running `ArchiveSweeper.evaluate` with `graceSeconds: 0` — the same
evidence pass, not a path around it. `archivedFolder` (held folder if the sweeper has moved it,
else the worktree) is the single answer to "which bytes", because the row, the header, the footer
and the cap must not each pick their own.

Divergences fixed on the way: `archiveStatusLine` said "archived 4h" where the design and the new
Settings row both say "4h ago"; the delete confirm crammed its consequence and its way out into
one placeholder and dropped the undo clause to fit, where its own neighbour the restart confirm
already argues for the note; and the second-opinion chip was indistinguishable from a verdict.

Gate: the budget algorithm unit-tested by extracting the shipped `overBudget` out of the Swift and
compiling it — blocked-never-promoted at any age or overage, both caps, No cap, exactly-at-cap,
all-blocked — plus `FolderSize.format` proven byte-identical to `fmtSize` over seven probes. Then
`swift build` clean, and a driven instance over its control socket on a seeded repo of five real
archived worktrees: the Synth tab's four rows, the project tab's list (ages, sizes, verdict chips,
the cut fifth row, the archive-wide footer), ⌘K's project-scoped `Archived · 5` drilling into
Restore / Delete worktree now, and the confirm frame with Cancel preselected. An independent
static audit against `working.html` found the three divergences above; they are fixed.

Both design files and the native app. Design gate: the policy unit-tested against the real source
pulled out of the design file — grace, the invariant (blocked never becomes eligible however old
or over budget, and is never called early), both caps, No cap, the switch, Never, the
second-opinion rule, and the bulk brake at 5 — 18 assertions. Then archive → restore → delete →
undo driven in the page, every knob exercised against the verdicts downstream of it, per-project
scoping checked in Settings and ⌘K, light and dark, no console errors, and the `working.html` /
`big-picture-design.html` subset diff.
## A waiting build gets a button in the sidebar foot, in the mark's own hue

The Settings foot button dropped its `⌘,` hint. The shortcut is unchanged and still discoverable in
two places that exist for exactly that (⌘? and the ⌘K row for Settings) — the hint was paying rent on
the one row of shell chrome that is always on screen to teach something you learn once.

That freed the foot to hold the thing that actually needs standing presence. The update used to speak
in a notification card as well as a Settings → About row, and both were wrong for a fact that persists
for days: the card is dismissible, and About is a place you only visit once you already suspect there
is a build waiting. Dismiss the card and a downloaded build left no visible trace anywhere in the
shell. So `Restart to update` appears directly above Settings for as long as a build sits unapplied,
carrying the version in the slot `⌘,` used to occupy, and leaves only when the build is installed.

**The card is gone, and no toast replaces it.** A waiting build now never interrupts — not when the
download lands, not once a day afterwards, and no Notification Center banner while Synth is unfocused.
The deck is where *work* speaks: an agent needs you, a run failed, an operation finished. A version you
were not waiting for is none of those, and putting it there taught the deck to be read as
interruptible-by-housekeeping. Both remaining surfaces are pull. The daily-reminder timer, the
`Downloaded N days ago` line it wore, and the "Restarting to install" receipt all went with it — the
real app relaunches at that point, so there was never anyone left on screen to read the receipt. What
used to be argued in copy ("nothing here is urgent") is now structural.

**Above Settings, not below.** The foot is bottom-anchored, so it grows upward — Settings stays exactly
where it was and nothing the user was already aiming at moves. The keyboard cursor walks the foot as a
run of buttons rather than a hardcoded single one, so ↓ off the last worktree leaf now lands on the
update and then Settings, and ↵ on it runs the same `restartForUpdate` the ⌘K confirm frame does,
including the confirm when sessions are busy. One code path, three surfaces, no way for them to
disagree.

### The mark's hue, as a wash rather than a slab

A filled brand-coloured button was built first and rejected on sight. It is the loudest thing in a
shell made almost entirely of hairlines and washes, and it is shouting on behalf of housekeeping —
the build is already downloaded and installs itself the next time you quit. Restarting is a shortcut,
not a price, and the control should not be sized like a price.

So it takes the shape the rest of the shell already uses for "this row is in a state": a tinted fill
and a 0.5px hairline, one step up from the Settings button beside it (full-strength `--ink` label at
600, against Settings' quieter tier). The hue is the icon's champagne, which keeps it clear of every
colour already spoken for — copper accent, slate-blue needs-input, green finished, red error.

Like `--accent` it flips per theme rather than holding one hex, and both halves of the flip were
forced by the same problem in opposite directions. A pale champagne tint over a near-white panel is
no tint at all, so light uses the hue taken down to `--update-ink` #7f6130 (5.2:1 on the sidebar).
Tinting *dark* with that instead goes olive the moment it lands on the dark sidebar. And the mark's
own #eee0cd, used as dark's tint, reads plain grey — its saturation is too low to survive 13% alpha —
so dark's `--update-rgb` restores the chroma (236,206,155) while the glyph stays the mark itself.
Pushed one step further (232,196,132) it turns yellow, which is the boundary this sits just inside.

Three variants lost: a champagne slab (right in dark, washed-out cream in light), a charcoal slab —
the icon literally, which is excellent on light and sinks into the dark sidebar — and an outline with
no fill, which reads as disabled. The pair-flipping slab that would have solved the first two was the
recommendation and was passed over deliberately: the point here is restraint, not contrast.

The cursor ring is the same primitive every other navigable row uses, drawn in the row's own hue
instead of the accent, because the shared rule swaps the background for a neutral wash and would have
taken this row's tint with it.
## The other two agents get the same treatment, and only one of them needs a fix

Having done Claude Code, the same measurement was run against opencode and `agy`. They look like the
same problem and are three different problems, which is the finding worth keeping.

### opencode: the machinery already works, the light values were too pale

The first result was a **harness artefact**, and it is the same one recorded on 2026-07-27 for Claude
Code — worth writing down twice because it is so easy to walk into. Driven under a bare pty, opencode
painted `#0a0a0a` over everything on a light surface: apparently the identical "dark theme on a light
card" disaster. It is not. opencode *asks* — one OSC 11 background query, one OSC 10, and DEC mode
2031 — and a pty that records output without answering anything makes it fall back to dark. Once the
harness replied the way ghostty does (`\e]11;rgb:f7f7/f8f8/fafa\e\\`), opencode painted light.

It goes further than Claude Code: fed ghostty's `CSI ? 997 ; 1 n`, a **running** opencode re-themed.
So there is nothing for Synth to keep in step — no rewriting, no appearance path. `theme: "system"`
does what its name says.

What was wrong was the values. Measured against the surfaces opencode paints *for itself* — it never
lets the terminal's background show through, so `backgroundElement` `#f5f5f5` is the reference rather
than `TerminalTheme`'s — its light half left **`textMuted` at 3.17:1** (placeholder text, comments,
horizontal rules) and **`accent`/`warning` at 2.52:1** (headings, keywords, the effort indicator),
with eight more ink values under 4.5:1. Synth now installs `~/.config/opencode/themes/synth.json` and
points `tui.json` at it: **11 values deepened**, hue kept, and the dark half copied through untouched
— which `t25` asserts by extracting opencode's own theme out of its binary and comparing, rather than
by claiming it.

Two things about opencode's design shaped the result. **A partial theme crashes it** —
`undefined is not an object (evaluating 'a.background.a')` — so this is necessarily a fork of
opencode's default and will drift; the gate renders a real opencode against the file so a stale key
fails loudly. And **opencode paints its own opaque background**, which means an opencode pane sits out
the window's translucency. Noted, not fixed: that is the theme's `background`, and setting it to match
would be guessing at a colour the terminal composites rather than owns.

The gate also caught a bug in itself that is worth recording. Its first drift check failed, claiming
Synth's theme was missing `backgroundMenu` and `selectedListItemText` — because the extraction
anchored on `lightStep11:"#8a8a8a"`, which **31 of opencode's 33 built-in themes share**, and so
compared Synth's fork against a theme it was never based on. Only one built-in defines all 52 keys;
opencode's own default defines 50, so those two carry a built-in fallback and omitting them is exactly
as safe as shipping the default. Anchoring on a *pair* of values fixed it.

### agy: nothing to configure, so nothing was shipped

`agy` 1.1.x asks the terminal **nothing** — no OSC 11, no OSC 10, no DEC 2031 — has no theme setting
in `--help`, no theme subcommand, and no theme key in `~/.gemini/antigravity-cli/settings.json`, which
holds only `trustedWorkspaces`. It paints hard-coded truecolor. `GetThemeMode` and `THEME_DEVICE` do
appear in the binary and are a red herring: they sit among Chrome DevTools-protocol strings from the
embedded browser. There is no `AppleInterfaceStyle` read either.

So there is no file to write and no setting to adopt, and the honest output is a measurement rather
than a fix. On Synth's light surface: **`#4285f4` at 3.35:1** (the Google-blue banner), **`#d0d0d0` at
1.45:1** (ink inside a fenced code block, found in a real conversation), and **`#9296a1` at 2.78:1**.

That last one is Synth's own, and it is a dead end rather than an oversight. `#9296a1` is ANSI slot 7,
which agy uses as dim ink for headings. `TerminalTheme` keeps slots 7 and 15 light on purpose because
a TUI paints them *under* dark ink, and `t13`'s fixture asserts exactly that. Darkening slot 7 to
`#767676` would carry agy's ink at 4.5:1 and simultaneously drop t13's "slot 7 as a fill under default
ink" from 4.5 to **3.64**. One palette cannot serve both sides — the same conclusion the 256-colour
ramp reached, arrived at from the opposite direction. Dark is not clean either: agy's own `#666666`
renders the signed-in address at 3.23:1.

`t26` therefore pins rather than gates, at 2.70:1, and its fixture is the three screens agy draws with
no model call — the banner, the `?` shortcuts overlay, the `/` command palette. A conversation would
be richer, and the code-fence number came from one, but agy keeps conversation content in a
per-conversation SQLite database with a private schema; authoring that is a worse foundation for a gate
than three screens it renders from nothing.

### One role was added to the shared measurement, and it earns its place

`ccontrast` now distinguishes **fill** from chrome: a block element (`█ ▀ ▐`) in a flat colour is not a
control or a boundary, it *is* a surface, and WCAG asks nothing of the contrast between two adjacent
surfaces. opencode draws its input panel as a band of `▀` one shade off its own background (1.09:1)
and reads perfectly well; Claude Code's mascot is a `▐███▌` silhouette. Fills are reported, never
gated. The alternative was per-glyph exemptions in two suites, and this way the rule is written once.
It also retired one of the Claude Code overrides: `clawd_body` was being deepened to clear 3:1 as
chrome, which — the mascot being brand artwork and a fill besides — it never owed.

## Correction: agy does have a colour-scheme setting, and its default is still the right one

The entry above says `agy` has no theme setting. That is wrong, and the correction is worth more than
the original claim because it changes *why* Synth ships nothing for it.

agy has a **Color Scheme** setting with eight options and a live preview. It is invisible from the
outside — not in `--help`, not a subcommand, and absent from `settings.json` until it is changed — and
lives in the interactive `/config` panel, persisting as a plain `colorScheme` string. Looking for a
config key and a CLI flag and finding neither is not the same as there being no setting, which is the
mistake that was made.

So Synth *could* write it. It does not, because **no option is accessible and the default is the best
of them.** Measured on a real conversation, 168 runs, on Synth's light surface:

| scheme | failing runs | worst overall | worst *text* |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `terminal` (default) | 2 | 1.45 | **1.45** — code-fence ink |
| `light` | 4 | 1.29 — separator | 2.80 |
| `colorblind-friendly light` | 4 | 1.29 — separator | 2.80 |
| `solarized light` | 5 | 1.15 — separator | 2.89 |

The reason `terminal` wins is the part worth keeping: in that mode agy draws most of its UI from the
terminal's **ANSI palette** — the set `TerminalTheme` already tunes to 7:1 on the light surface. Synth's
palette is better than agy's own light theme, so switching schemes throws the palette work away. It
does repair the code fence, and in exchange breaks the separator to 1.29:1 and adds two more failures
in the high 2s: one bad value traded for four.

Which leaves the honest position unchanged, for a better reason. The remaining light-mode defects are
`#d0d0d0` code-fence ink at **1.45:1** and `#4285f4` at 3.35:1, both hard-coded truecolor and upstream's
to fix, plus `#9296a1` at 2.78:1 — Synth's own ANSI slot 7, the dead end already recorded above.

`t26` now drives a fourth screen, the settings panel, and prints the Color Scheme row it finds. That is
the tripwire the original conclusion lacked: if agy ships an accessible scheme, or changes which one is
default, it shows up in the suite's output instead of going unnoticed. Two incidental notes from
getting there: `solarized-light` with a hyphen is rejected (the value is `solarized light`, and agy
validates the key and shows a settings error, so a wrong write is loud rather than silent), and
escaping the slash palette leaves the `/` it was opened with in the input — typing straight into it
produces `//config` and matches nothing, which is why the fixture sends backspaces first.

## Two loose ends closed: the subagent hues are dual-use, and an ANSI light base was measured and declined

Asking "is that everything?" turned up one unverified claim and one lever dismissed too early.

### The subagent hues are a badge fill as well as ink, and deepening them helped both

Eight `*_FOR_SUBAGENTS_ONLY` tokens were deepened without ever being seen on screen. That is exactly
how the `diffAddedWord` mistake happened — a token that reads like a foreground turning out to be a
fill — so it was worth settling rather than assuming.

They cannot be rendered from a transcript: a resumed conversation shows a subagent tree
(`⏺ Explore(...)`, `├ Worker 0 · 0 tool uses`) without ever reaching for these colours, and three
concurrent subagents do not either. They are assigned during a *live* run. So the answer came from the
code instead. `bht(agentType)` maps a subagent to its token, and one call site returns
`{text, bgColor: bht(...) ?? "cyan_FOR_SUBAGENTS_ONLY"}` — a **background**. The badge component then
resolves its foreground as `textColor ?? (color ? "inverseText" : undefined)`, and that call site
passes no `textColor`, so it is **white on the token**. The same token is separately used as plain ink
for the rule beside the badge.

Dual use, and both directions want the same thing — darker:

| use | before | after |
| --- | --- | --- |
| as ink on `#f7f8fa` | 2.75–3.53 | 4.60–4.64 |
| as a badge fill under white | 2.93–3.76 (all failing) | 4.89–4.93 |

So the overrides were right, and for a reason that had not been checked. `t24` now asserts it: every
overridden badge fill must clear 4.5:1 against white as well as against the surface. That is the
check that would have caught `diffAddedWord`, generalised — and it cuts the opposite way here, which
is the argument for measuring rather than reasoning from a token's name.

### `base: "light-ansi"` is strictly better on contrast, and was declined on how it looks

Claude Code picks its syntax highlighter from the *resolved base* by substring: `ansi` → ansi,
`dark` → Monokai, otherwise GitHub. `base` accepts the picker's ANSI variants, so a custom theme can
say `light-ansi` — and then the syntax highlighter, the diff renderer and even the hard-coded
`#5769f7` all route through the terminal's sixteen slots, which is the palette `TerminalTheme` already
tunes to 7:1 and `t13` already gates. Measured on the same fixture with highlighting on:

| configuration | failing | worst | nature |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `light` + 19 overrides (shipped) | 6 | 3.52 | all **text** |
| `light-ansi`, no overrides | 2 | 2.19 | both **chrome** |
| `light-ansi` + 18 overrides | 1 | 2.19 | **chrome** |

Both ANSI variants have **zero failing text runs**, and the first needs no override table at all —
drift-proof against Claude Code changing its theme, and simpler to explain than anything else here.

Declined anyway, deliberately, and the reason is not contrast: ANSI-only is sixteen colours, so light
mode would read as visibly flatter than dark. Light and dark ceasing to look like the same app is a
worse outcome than six text runs between 3.52 and 4.21 — poor, but legible, and none of them the
unreadable case this work started from. Recorded here with the numbers so the option is a decision
already taken rather than one to rediscover. (`subtle` is the override that has to come off in an ANSI
base: `#707070` lands on a slot-7 fill at 1.67:1, which is what the third row costs.)
## Tabs are chips on a rail, and a split is a hairline tray

The tab strip stops being one solid band welded to the window. Each tab is a 28px chip on a 42px
rail: no seams, no bottom bar, no fill running out to the window edge. The open tab lifts off the
rail as a card — `--raised` behind a hairline, with a small shadow — and that elevation is the whole
of "open". An unopened tab is its label and nothing else.

Three chip directions were built live in the shell and flipped between: **inset** (elevation on a
bare rail), **track** (one recessed well with a thumb sliding between segments), and **focus** (only
what is on screen spends horizontal width — hidden sessions collapse to an icon and their signal).
Inset won. Track was the most Mac-native and the only one with real motion to switch, but it is the
tightest on space: everything shares one hugging container, so it truncates first. Focus solved the
real failure the exercise surfaced — at a 900px window the old strip ellipsizes *every* name to
nothing — but you cannot read an icon-only strip at a glance when half the sessions share a terminal
icon.

### The bleed goes away, and that is the point

The old strip's first tab carried its fill out under the sidebar's rounded top-right corner, through
a `.tab-bleed` block masked to exactly the wedge the curve leaves uncovered — a whole element and a
`radial-gradient` mask existing so one fill did not stop in a hard square short of a curve. Chips
are inset off every edge, so no fill ever reaches the seam. The element, the mask, the two `:has()`
rules that toggled it and the `--sidebar-w` arithmetic are all deleted, and the strip loses its
bottom rule as well: nothing needs a seam to stop at.

### A split is a tray with no fill, and the fill was the mistake

An on-screen split's members fold into one contiguous run, and how that run reads as one thing was
its own round: a **tray** enclosing it, a copper **bracket** under it, a **lead** glyph stating the
geometry once at the head, or the run **merged** into a single chip carrying the geometry plus a
stack of member icons.

Tray won, but its first version was a recessed fill, and that was wrong for a reason worth keeping:
on this strip *a background is what open means*. Filling the tray hands every unfocused member the
one signal reserved for exactly one tab. The tray is now a hairline container with nothing behind
it, so a member sits on the bare rail like any other unopened tab and the active member is the only
card in the strip.

Merged is the direction to remember if the strip ever runs out of room. It is the only shell that
gets *cheaper* as a split grows — with a four-pane split every other tab in the branch keeps its full
name — and it was rejected on signal, not on space: a non-active member loses its indicator, so a
pane going to needs-input behind the merged chip says nothing. Only unread survived, as a dot on the
member icon.

### `min-width: 0` is why the members refused to shrink

The members held ~390px while the lone tabs beside them were squeezed to bare icons, and it was not
a flex-grow question. `.tab-group` is itself a flex item, and with `min-width: auto` its floor is its
own min-content — the sum of its members' longest unbreakable words. A lone `.tab` never hit that
floor because it sets `min-width: 34px` explicitly, which *replaces* the automatic minimum. Zeroing
the tray's min-width puts a member on exactly the same terms as every other tab, which is the whole
premise: a member IS a tab, so it should shrink and ellipsize like one.

### Prototyping it

`working.html` gained a temporary split-layout seeder — 1, 2↔, 2↕, 3, 4 — that builds the pane tree
straight from the branch's sessions, because the app grows a split one pane at a time and there was
no way to look at a 2×2 in a couple of clicks. Its fractions are deliberately uneven (0.56, 0.62,
0.54/0.55, 0.52/0.46): an all-halves screen is the one arrangement the 8px pane map draws exactly,
and seeding it would flatter the map into looking more faithful than it is. Above two panes is where
tray's cost shows — the run eats the width the strip exists to spend on other sessions' names.

## The rail stops being deeper than the run it holds

Feedback on the new chip strip: the vertical space around a split's tab group felt like a lot more than
the horizontal space. Measured, in tabs mode with a two-pane split:

| | |
|---|---|
| tray → its member chips | 2px, all four sides |
| tray → the lone tab beside it | 3px |
| tray → top and bottom of the rail | 5px |
| rail → left/right window edge | 8px |

Nothing is asymmetric *inside* the tray. The vertical air is the rail's own: a 32px tray centred in a
42px rail leaves 5px above and below, against a 3px gap to its neighbour. `.tabstrip` goes to **36px** —
the 32px tray plus 2px, which is the same 2px the tray gives its own members. The clearance around the
run now matches the clearance inside it, and a lone tab (28px, no tray) gets 4px.

### Why the tray's own 2px stayed

The other reading of "basically no spacing" was `.tab-group { padding: 0 2px }`, making the tray 28px so
its silhouette matched every other chip in the strip. That was rejected: the tray and the active member
both carry an `inset 0 0 0 0.5px` hairline, so at zero vertical padding those two hairlines land on top
of each other and the tray survives only in the 2px side gaps. The tray is the whole of "these three are
one split" — it can't be the thing that disappears.

### The top row goes out of alignment, and that is the trade

The rail centres its contents, so everything in it — chips, tray, `+`, the relocated PR chip — rises 3px:
centreline 43.5 → 40.5. The traffic lights and the sidebar toggle sit at 47.5. They were already 4px
apart; they are 7px apart now. Nothing can close that from the other side: the sidebar top row could come
down to meet the chips, but the real traffic lights can't — AppKit fixes their vertical position, and the
faux ones in the design files exist to mimic where AppKit puts them. So the choice was a rail that hugs
its tabs or a top row that lines up across the divider, and the rail won, because the rail is what you
look at every time you glance up and the divider already breaks the row.

## The simulator's comment mode is offered: the app-exit was one `%s`

**The toggle withheld on 2026-08-03 is restored**, with the target chip that names the receiving session
while the mode is on. Delivering a comment from an unowned simulator no longer exits the app, because the
exit was never in the spawn, the pane swap, the ownership cascade or the timing — all of which the last
session had already cleared. It was the log line that runs *after* all of them succeed:

```
NSLog("Synth: %s comment delivered …", subjectKindLabel, …)
```

`%s` wants a C string. A Swift `String` reaches varargs bridged to `NSString`, and a short one is a
**tagged pointer** — a value that was never an address — so CoreFoundation's formatter hands `strlen` a
pointer to nowhere and the read faults. `NSLog("%s", "simulator")` reproduces it in four lines outside
Synth.

Nothing about it looked like a crash from outside, and that is the more expensive finding: **libghostty
statically links sentry-native, whose Breakpad handler claims the task's Mach exception ports** at
`ghostty_init` — which a comment in `SynthApp` already said out loud. A Mach exception preempts POSIX
signals, so the fault never became a `SIGSEGV`: `CrashReporter`'s marker was never written, the OS wrote no
report, and the process simply left with `exit(1)` about four seconds after the comment was sent. Every
crash in Synth is currently invisible in exactly this way. The benign `notification refused (Claude
finished)` line that always preceded the exit was a coincidence, not a clue — the trigger is a *successful*
delivery, so whether the spawned agent then lives or dies has nothing to do with it.

Found by attaching lldb to the reproduction the last session deliberately left behind and asking for a
backtrace at the fault, which is the argument for leaving one behind.

**Both rungs of the ladder carried it, not just the unowned one.** The same `%s` sits on rung one's
success line — the browser's shipped path — where it would have fired the moment a comment was delivered
to a live owner. Both are now driven end to end on a real device: unowned (spawn → adopt → boot-and-wait,
delivered at t+4s) and owned-and-live (delivered synchronously), with each comment arriving in the agent's
own transcript carrying its element line and its screenshot.

**The hit test moved off the main actor.** A click resolved the accessibility element synchronously on the
main actor, inside a process-wide gate whose worst case is an 8s request timeout plus a recovery path of
two 30s shell timeouts and a 2s sleep — around 78s of frozen window, the user's terminals and agent panes
with it, from one click. The click now opens the composer and captures its evidence at once (measured
0.12s) and the anchor lands when it lands (0.37s); the composer says it is still reading rather than
claiming there is nothing there. `send` waits for a hit test still in flight instead of shipping a comment
that would have said "nothing in the accessibility tree" — the anchor is the whole point of a located
comment. The request is a blocking one, so it goes to a Dispatch queue rather than the cooperative pool it
would otherwise hold a thread of hostage.

## `simulator_describe` answers in landscape, because the projection is confirmed rather than assumed

Landscape `describe` refused, because AXP reports element frames in the interface's coordinate space while
`tap` addresses the display, which never rotates. The projection between them is one quarter turn over a
transposed extent and was never the hard part. The hard part is that **nothing on this platform will say
which way up an app laid itself out**: the guest publishes no orientation read-back, an app is free to
ignore a rotation (Settings is portrait-locked), and the tree's own root frame says *landscape* without
saying which landscape. What Synth last asked for is write-only state, and the previous bug was built out
of trusting it.

So the space is **confirmed against the device on every read**. That is possible only because the two
halves of this API disagree: frames come back in the interface's space and `objectAtPoint:` goes in in the
display's. Predict where an element's centre lands on the display under a candidate space, ask what is
there, and accept the candidate only if what comes back is that element. The root frame rules out the
candidates it cannot fit and the last-set orientation only orders the survivors, so a normal read costs one
extra hit test (~90ms describe on Settings, ~110ms on landscape Safari) and a portrait-locked app is read
as portrait however many rotations it was sent. The point hit-test verb pays nothing: the caller's own
point already resolved to an element, and only a space that puts that point inside that element's frame can
be real.

Three things fell out of doing it, each of which the previous attempt's notes had wrong or had not reached.
"The interface's space turns opposite to the orientation's name" was false — both landscape transforms map
0..1 onto 0..1, so out-of-range centres came from the divisor, not the direction. "A hit test at a reported
centre came back with a different element" was a consequence of that same divisor, not a missing origin.
And **a tree can be in two coordinate spaces at once**: with the keyboard up in landscape, Safari's
elements come back in the interface's space and every key in the display's, as flat siblings, with no
window, parent or presenter pid to separate them. Elements are therefore assigned a space each, and the
ones both spaces could hold are settled one hit test apiece — a cost paid only on a screen that is really
mixed.

The check no longer asserts the refusal. It taps the reported centre of Safari's address field in landscape
and requires the keyboard to appear, taps the keyboard's own `x` key and requires the field to read `x`,
and taps Settings' General row while the device has been asked for landscape and requires About to appear.
"The screen changed" is deliberately not the assertion, and there is now evidence why: against a
deliberately broken projection, `tap-changed-screen` passed at 34% while the tap landed on Apple
Intelligence & Siri instead of General. 48 assertions pass live plus forced-degraded.

## A crash that left no trace, and why

The comment-mode exit was a `%s` in an `NSLog` format string holding a Swift `String`. A short Swift
string bridges to an `NSString` **tagged pointer** — a value that was never an address — so
CoreFoundation's formatter handed `strlen` a pointer to nowhere. lldb on the live reproduction:
`_platform_strlen` ← `__CFStringAppendFormatCore` ← `_NSLogv` ← `CommentDelivery.swift`. Reproducible in
four lines of standalone Swift.

Mine, introduced while extracting the delivery ladder: the original log line named the browser in its
literal, and adding a `subjectKindLabel` parameter for the shared version is what introduced the format
specifier. **The same line sits on rung 1**, the browser's shipped path, where it fires immediately on
delivery to a live owner — so the extraction broke the browser's comment mode too, not only the
simulator's. `%@` throughout; there are no other `%s` sites in the app.

**Why it presented as a silent `exit(1)`.** libghostty statically links sentry-native, whose Breakpad
handler claims the task's Mach exception ports at `ghostty_init`. Mach exceptions preempt POSIX signals,
so the fault never became `SIGSEGV`, `CrashReporter`'s signal handlers never ran, its marker file was never
written, and no `.ips` was produced. `SynthApp` already knows about this and orders launch specifically to
beat it — Ghostty starts first so PostHog's Mach handler layers on top and chains back to Breakpad rather
than letting Breakpad swallow everything.

That ordering does not help on the **dev channel**, because `Analytics.bootstrap` returns early there
(unless `SYNTH_ANALYTICS_DEBUG=1`), so the Mach handler that was supposed to rescue the signal is never
installed at all. On the dev build — the one the author runs all day — a crash therefore leaves no marker,
no crash report and no log line: it looks like a clean exit. That is not caused by this feature and it is
narrower than "every crash in Synth is invisible", but it is worth its own fix, and it cost hours of this
investigation before lldb settled it.

## The check now claims only what it tests

An independent review audited the self-check itself and found the count overstated the evidence: about ten
assertions were literally `report(true, …)`, the four headline `*-live` lines were only the `else` branch of
"the source reported no degradation", `layer-accepts-frames` could not fail because the layer was never in a
window so its status stayed `.unknown`, and `tap-changed-screen` keyed off a surface seed that a ticking
clock moves. Fair, and the fix is partly honesty rather than more assertions:

- The four `*-live` lines are now `*-path-resolved`. They assert that every private path resolved at start,
  which is worth asserting and is not the same as working. Frames arriving, a tap landing, a tree
  round-tripping and a device turning are each asserted by name elsewhere.
- `tap-changed-screen` is now `tap-produced-a-new-frame`, because that is what it proves.
- `layer-accepts-frames` puts the view in a real offscreen window, so the layer enters a render cycle and
  reports `rendering`. Detached it could only ever report `unknown`, and "not failed" was unfalsifiable.

The landscape work went further in the same direction: its assertions name a **consequence** that must be
absent before and present after — "Clear text appeared", "About appeared" — never "the screen changed". That
distinction was proved to matter: against a deliberately broken projection the consequence assertions all
fail, while a screen-changed assertion still passed at 34.4% with the tap landing on the wrong row.

### A device difference, found by running on a second model

The landscape round trip was verified on iPhone 16 and then failed on iPhone 16 Pro — not because the
projection was wrong (the hit test round-tripped **0.0000 off** on both) but because the assertion's chosen
consequence was a keyboard key, and **whether the software keyboard's keys reach the accessibility tree
varies by device**: present on iPhone 16, absent on iPhone 16 Pro on the same iOS. The tap had landed, the
field was active, and only the keyboard's enumeration was missing.

So the projection's own proof no longer depends on the keyboard, and the mixed-space keyboard case reports
`INFO … skipping` rather than failing — a device difference is not a defect. The check is now run on two
models as a matter of course; both pass 46 assertions plus forced degradation.

## Round three: the verb each earlier fix missed

A third independent review, on a third device model, returned "ship with these fixes first". Its headline
finding was the previous two rounds' finding surviving on the one verb they had each missed, and it is worth
recording because the pattern repeats: a rule enforced verb-by-verb gets enforced almost everywhere.

Against a device **shut down underneath a live session**, it measured `simulator.type` answering `ok` four
times out of four, `pressButton` answering `ok`, and `tap` alternating — three honest answers out of eight.
Three causes, all now closed:

- `type` had its own path with no readiness check at all.
- `checkReady()` **consumed** the latched failure on its way out, so the next verb saw a clean slate. It now
  peeks; only reopening a session clears the latch, because a fresh session is the only evidence that
  whatever failed has been fixed.
- Readiness was the wrong question. A HID client object outlives the device that gave it a mach port, so
  "we have a client" was true of a dead device — which is why the *first* verb after a shutdown still
  reported success even once the latch worked. Every input verb now asks whether the **device** is there,
  through the same liveness check the screenshot verb already used, cached for 250 ms so a sixty-moves-a-second
  drag does not pay an XPC round trip per move. A failure is never cached, so recovery is immediate.

Re-measured after the fix: 4 types, 2 button presses, 8 taps, `describe` and `screenshot` — **all refused**.

`describe` was asserting a false fact on a dead device — *"its accessibility server answered, so something is
on screen that it will not describe — launch an app and try again"* — advice that sends an agent into a loop.
It goes through liveness now too.

**Comment mode was handing a stale frame to an agent as current evidence.** `captureCurrentFrame()` falls
back to the last frame, which is right for a pane re-presenting during a blip and wrong for anything handed
to someone else as proof, because the IOSurface outlives the device. There is now a `captureVerifiedFrame()`
for evidence; the screenshot verb and comment mode use it, and the pane keeps the forgiving one. Measured
before: 255 KB of pre-shutdown screen written and offered as the current screen. After: nothing written, an
honest notice, and no composer — a comment with no screenshot and no anchor is honest about having nothing
and useless to whoever receives it.

Two more: the **self-check could shut down a device it had not booted**, because `bootedByUs` came from a
state read rather than from `boot()`'s own outcome — the same finding an earlier round fixed in the app and
this file then reintroduced, with `SimulatorBootOutcome` sitting there unused. And a typo'd
`SYNTH_SIM_CHECK_UDID` silently retargeted the check at "first iPhone by newest runtime" and then shut *that*
down; a miss is now fatal, and matching is case-insensitive. `SimulatorCommentModeController.teardown()` also
had no caller, so abandoned composer screenshots were never reclaimed.

### The coverage gap is a failure now, not a footnote

The mixed-space keyboard case — the most intricate code in the feature — was skipped with an `INFO` on
devices whose software keyboard does not reach the accessibility tree, and the check still said PASS. That is
the same species of assertion this check has already been caught writing, so it fails now and names what was
not exercised.

Chasing which devices can exercise it corrected the earlier note: it is **not** a model rule and **not** a
runtime rule. Measured present on one iPhone 16 / iOS 18.4, absent on an iPhone 16 Pro on the *same* runtime,
and absent on a different iPhone 16 on 18.6 — which points at whether that device presents a software
keyboard at all, per-device persisted state. The check names that and says to run somewhere it shows.

Green on a keyboard-bearing device: **48 assertions plus forced degradation**.
